Computer Science & Algorithms

20! ≈ 2.4 × 10¹⁸

That's how many routes a brute-force TSP solver must check for just 20 cities — at 1 billion operations per second, it would take 76 years. Factorials are the reason we need clever algorithms, not just fast computers.

  • Sorting: 52! ≈ 8×10⁶⁷ possible orderings of a deck of cards — more than atoms in the observable universe (10⁸⁰).
  • Feature selection: choosing 5 features from 20 candidates = C(20,5) = 15,504 — that's why exhaustive search is rarely practical.
  • RSA encryption: factoring a 2048-bit number is hard because the search space is combinatorially vast.

AI & Machine Learning

Beta(8, 4)

After 8 clicks and 4 purchases on your A/B test, Bayesian inference uses Beta distributions to estimate the true conversion rate — no p-values needed, just continuous updating.

  • LLM sampling: GPT chooses the next token from a multinomial distribution — the normalization constant involves factorials of vocabulary size.
  • Thompson sampling: uses Beta posteriors to balance exploration vs exploitation in real-time ad placement.
  • Cross-validation: C(10,3) = 120 ways to split data into folds — the basis of robust model evaluation.

Statistics & Data Science

p = 0.05 ≠ 95%

A p-value of 0.05 does NOT mean there's a 95% chance the effect is real. It means: if there were no effect, you'd see data this extreme 5% of the time. Bayesian methods using Beta posteriors give you what you actually want: the probability the effect is real.

  • Clinical trials: testing a drug on 200 patients uses binomial power analysis to determine sample size.
  • Chi-squared test: with k=10 degrees of freedom, the distribution is Gamma(5) — that's what your stats software computes.
  • Permutation test: assigning 12 patients to two groups of 6 gives C(12,6) = 924 random assignments — the exact null distribution.

Physics & Engineering

7!! = 105

Feynman diagrams — the computational backbone of quantum field theory — count contractions using double factorials. Each diagram represents a term in an infinite series that describes how particles interact.

  • Wick's theorem: computing n-field contractions requires (n-1)!! pairings — for 7 fields, that's 105 Feynman diagrams.
  • Black body radiation: Planck's law for photon statistics involves Gamma functions — this is how we understand stars.
  • Quantum harmonic oscillator: the n-th energy level normalization uses (2n)!! — connecting factorials to quantum energy quantization.

Biology & Bioinformatics

10³⁰⁰ conformations

Levinthal's paradox: a protein with 100 amino acids has ~10³⁰⁰ possible conformations. If it tried one per nanosecond, it would take longer than the age of the universe to fold by random search. Yet it folds in milliseconds — nature solved the combinatorial explosion.

  • COVID variants: the spike protein has ~1,270 amino acids. Each position can mutate to ~19 alternatives — the combinatorial space is staggering.
  • K-mer counting: counting 3-mers in a 20-nucleotide sequence explores C(20,3) = 1,140 positions — fundamental to genome assembly.
  • Allele frequencies: Bayesian models use Beta distributions to track how gene frequencies evolve in populations.

Everyday Life

2²⁵⁶ keys

Your WiFi password uses WPA2 with 2²⁵⁶ possible keys — that's more than the number of atoms in the universe. Even with every computer on Earth working together, brute-forcing it would take longer than the heat death of the cosmos.

  • Lottery: picking 6 numbers from 49 gives C(49,6) = 13,983,816 possible tickets — your odds are about 1 in 14 million.
  • PIN security: a 4-digit PIN has 10⁴ = 10,000 combinations. After 3 wrong attempts, your phone locks — that's why 2-factor authentication matters.
  • Seating 8 guests around a round table: 7! = 5,040 arrangements — explains why the host needs a seating chart algorithm.
